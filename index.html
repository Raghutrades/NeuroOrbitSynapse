Why this works

What happens in your brain

When one neuron repeatedly helps another fire, the synapse between them gets stronger (Hebbian learning, in the lab called long-term potentiation). The connection gets more receptors and a bigger contact area, so the same signal passes more easily next time. With weeks of practice, the wiring also gets insulated with myelin, which makes the signal faster. Connections that go unused get pruned, which is why a neuron on this map cools down when you skip days.

Why it beats blind repetition
  • Attention gates change. Focused effort releases chemicals like acetylcholine and dopamine that tell the brain "keep this". Distracted reps change very little, so a 20 minute focused block can beat two hours of scrolling and working.
  • Spacing and sleep. Memory consolidation happens between sessions, mostly in sleep. One session a day, repeated, builds more than one long cram.
  • Effort becomes automatic. With repetition, control shifts from the effortful planning areas (prefrontal cortex) toward habit circuits (basal ganglia). The goal needs less willpower each week.
  • Visible progress feeds motivation. Seeing a dot light up gives a small reward signal, which makes you more likely to come back tomorrow.
  • A fixed cue removes decisions. Deciding "when X happens, I do Y" (an implementation intention) is shown to raise follow-through, because the choice is already made.

Blind following means the same reps with no cue, no focus, no feedback, and no review. This page makes you set a cue, prepare, focus, rate the session honestly, and notice which goals are cooling.

Breaking a bad habit (scrolling, smoking, drinking)

A habit is a strongly wired pathway, and willpower cannot delete a synapse. What weakens it is not firing it: connections that go unused lose strength (long-term depression) and, over time, unused branches get pruned. At the same time, a replacement behaviour needs to be strengthened, so the urge has somewhere else to go. That is why a quit goal shrinks as clean days grow.

Cravings come in waves that rise and pass if you do not act on them, so every resisted urge is a rep of weakening. The old pathway fades but is never fully erased, which is why slips happen and why a plan for the urge ("when it hits, I will ...") matters. A slip here re-strengthens the old path a little (it costs about 2 days of progress) and does not erase your history. If you drink heavily, check with a doctor before stopping suddenly, because withdrawal can be dangerous.

How to use it
  • Set a cue for each goal in Edit (for example "after morning chai, at my desk").
  • Spend 2 to 10 minutes on prep: phone away, material open, one clear target for the session.
  • Run one focused block with the timer, then rate the quality 1 to 5, honestly.
  • Fire the neuron. Look at the map. Notice which goals are cooling and give them tomorrow's first slot.
  • If you miss a day, just restart the next day. One miss does not erase the wiring; a pattern of misses does.
Honest limits

This map is a metaphor. It cannot measure your synapses. The 100 day target is a sensible horizon, not a magic number: in a well known habit study (Lally et al., 2009), automaticity took about 66 days on average, with a range from roughly 18 to 254 days. Picturing a goal helps rehearsal, but it supports real practice and never replaces it.
